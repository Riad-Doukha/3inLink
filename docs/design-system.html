3inLink design system

One visual language for three surfaces: the eye-controlled screen a patient looks at, the mobile app nurses carry on the ward, and the landing page that explains the project. Built from the HCI and staff-app figures in the project brief.

Principles

Every choice below follows from who is using each screen and under what conditions.

  • Dark rooms come first.The system exists because camera-based tools fail in low light. The patient screen has a night mode that is dim, low-glare and never pure white. The care assistant app has one too, for night shifts.
  • Looking selects, a double blink sends.Gaze only previews a message. Nothing reaches staff until the patient blinks twice in quick succession. A single blink is ignored because people blink naturally. The design must make these two states impossible to confuse.
  • Eight messages, two phases.The same eight messages sit around the message panel in a 3×3 grid in both phases, and the staff app uses the same icons. Phase 1 runs on the current single-channel circuit, which detects up and down. Phase 2 runs on the new two-channel circuit with all four directions. Only the way the patient moves between tiles changes.
  • Never rely on color alone.Every state carries an icon, a label and a border change as well as a color. Red-green color blindness must not hide a state.
  • Three languages, equal rank.English, Arabic and French have the same size, weight and room. Arabic runs right to left. The gaze grid stays physical and never flips.

Patient screen

The interface the patient looks at: eight messages in a 3×3 matrix, with the message panel in the middle cell. It is delivered in two phases. Phase 1 runs on the current single-channel circuit, which detects up and down. Phase 2 runs on the new two-channel circuit that detects all four directions. Switch phase, move gaze with the buttons or the arrow keys, then double blink (press Space or the button twice) to confirm. Clicking a tile jumps straight to it, for this demo only.

  • Layout is a 3×3 matrix.Eight symbols surround the message panel, which takes the center cell. The gaze rests in the middle, so the patient reads the selected message without moving the eyes. Positions are the same in both phases.
  • Phase 1 reads two directions.The current single-channel circuit tells up from down (and a straight gaze, which does nothing), so gaze goes around the ring, one tile per glance: down moves clockwise, up counterclockwise. The classifier decides once per 3 s window, so each glance moves one tile. The ring has no end, so it wraps from the last tile to the first.
  • Phase 2 reads four.Up, down, left and right jump to the neighboring tile and wrap at the edges. The center is skipped, so the panel is never selected. Pain sits at the top, Nurse on the left, Water on the right and the bed request at the bottom.
  • Three states per tile.Idle: neutral border. Focus: green border and fill, icon turns green. Sent: red border and fill, icon turns red, and the panel reads "Sent to nursing staff" with patient ID and room.
  • A double blink confirms.One blink is ignored. Two blinks in quick succession send the message. After the first blink the panel reads "Blink 1 of 2" so the patient knows the second one will send. If it does not come within about a second, the first is forgotten.
  • Voice before send.On focus the message is spoken in the chosen language. The patient hears it, then double blinks. Language chips switch EN, AR and FR.
  • Gaze never sends.Moving the eyes across a tile only previews it. Moving to another tile clears a pending send state and a half-finished double blink, so a stray blink cannot repeat an old message.
  • Night mode is the default at night.No white surfaces, maximum 14:1 contrast on text. The panel is the darkest element so it does not glow in a dark room.
  • The AI assistant is a quiet pill.It shows that contextual, multilingual phrasing is active. It never changes which symbol is selected.

  • Header carries context.Ward label in mono, screen title, and a count badge of pending alerts. The badge is the only number in the header.
  • Card anatomy.Icon tile on the leading edge, message title, then two chips: patient ID (mono, tint) and room (mono, solid brand). Elapsed minutes at the trailing edge.
  • Pain is pinned and red.I Feel Pain stays at the top with a 2 px red border and a red tile. Other requests keep arrival order. Suggested: after 10 minutes unanswered, the elapsed time turns red.
  • Identifiers, not names.Cards show patient ID and room only, as in the brief. Names appear after the nurse opens a patient record.
  • Notifications.A new alert vibrates once and shows a banner with the same card. Pain uses a double vibration and stays on screen until opened.

Landing page

A proposed page structure. The hero is dark teal with the gaze grid, then four sections: how it works, who it is for, why it works in the dark, and project credits.

Connecting patients through their eyes

Your eyes can call for help.

3inLink is a low-cost wearable that turns eye movements and blinks into requests for the nursing team, for patients who cannot speak or move.

Research prototype under development. Not a certified medical device.

From a glance to an alert in four steps

  1. SenseTwo electrode channels read the eye's corneo-retinal potential.
  2. CleanAmplified and filtered at 0.1–35 Hz, battery powered to avoid 50 Hz mains noise.
  3. RecognizeUp and down glances are classified first on one channel, then left and right on two. A double blink confirms.
  4. AlertThe request reaches nurses with patient ID and room number.

Who it helps

After surgeryPatients who cannot speak or move for a while.
ParalysisPeople with severe mobility limits who still control their eyes.
ALS in emergenciesA fast, reliable way to reach staff when other tools fail.
Built for the ward at night

No camera. No light needed.

Camera-based systems depend on room lighting. 3inLink reads electrical signals from the skin, so patients can rest in a dark, quiet room and still ask for help.

Thirduni Project · Dr. A. Benazzouz · Mohamed Riad DoukhaOctober 2026
  • Claims stay inside the brief.Describe the system as a prototype for communication support. Do not promise diagnosis, treatment or clinical outcomes.
  • Numbered steps only for real sequences.The four-step strip is a pipeline in the brief (acquire, preprocess, process, command). Other sections use plain headings.
  • Show the interface early.The gaze grid in the hero is the same component as the patient screen, scaled down, so visitors see what patients see.

Rules for all three surfaces

ContrastText 4.5:1 or better, state borders 3:1 or better, in day and night.
State needs three signalsColor, border change and an icon or label. Never color alone.
RTLUse logical properties (start, end). Mirror layout and text. Do not mirror the gaze grid or direction arrows.
Language parityEN, AR and FR strings are written together, each checked by a native speaker, with room for the longest.
ErrorsSay what failed and what to do. Example: "Signal lost. Check the electrodes on the forehead and outer eye corners."
PrivacyStaff lists show patient ID and room. Avoid names and medical details on a lock screen.
